Campus Director Scenario Responses

Structured, values-driven answers focused on conflict mediation, team leadership, and collaborative growth.

1) How would you mediate two Fellows refusing to work together and protect an inclusive community?

  1. Pause escalation immediately: I would separate the active conflict by assigning short-term independent tasks for 24–48 hours, so project progress continues and tension does not spread.
  2. Gather facts through individual check-ins: I would meet each Fellow privately to hear their perspective, identify underlying interests, and understand whether values, communication, or role clarity are at the root of the disagreement.
  3. Re-anchor on shared mission and norms: I would reference cohort agreements (respect, curiosity, accountability, and inclusion) and clarify that disagreement is acceptable, but disrespect and disengagement are not.
  4. Run a structured mediation conversation: I would facilitate a meeting using a clear protocol:
    • Each person speaks uninterrupted for 3–5 minutes.
    • The other paraphrases what they heard.
    • Together we identify points of alignment and divergence.
    • We define objective project criteria (impact, feasibility, timeline, beneficiary needs) to evaluate options fairly.
  5. Agree on a decision mechanism: If consensus is not possible, I would apply a pre-defined tie-break method (pilot test, mentor advisory vote, or milestone-based experiment) so no one feels arbitrarily overruled.
  6. Create a written working agreement: We would document roles, deadlines, communication expectations, conflict reset steps, and accountability checkpoints.
  7. Restore broader cohort trust: Without disclosing private details, I would acknowledge the impact on morale and reinforce community standards in a cohort-wide check-in.
  8. Follow up and monitor: I would run 1:1 follow-ups after one week and two weeks, track collaboration behaviors, and escalate to formal intervention only if agreements are violated.

Outcome goal: conflict transformed into a learning moment, project momentum restored, and an inclusive environment where differing viewpoints are handled constructively.

2) Describe a time you managed a team or organized an event, the biggest challenge, and the outcome.

Situation

I led a team of 14 student leaders to organize a campus social impact showcase with 200+ expected attendees, six partner organizations, and a six-week timeline.

Task

My responsibility was to coordinate logistics, delegate ownership, and keep motivation high during midterm season when volunteer energy was dropping.

Action

The biggest challenge was inconsistent follow-through across committees. I introduced a lightweight operations system:

  • One shared timeline with weekly milestones.
  • Owners and backup owners for each deliverable.
  • 20-minute standups focused on blockers, not status monologues.
  • Motivation boosts through public recognition and small, visible wins each week.

Result

Attendance reached 260 participants, all partner organizations were represented, and we finished setup 45 minutes ahead of time. Post-event feedback averaged 4.7/5, and three volunteers later stepped into leadership roles because they felt supported and trusted.

3) Describe a disagreement with a co-leader or peer, how you handled it, and what you learned.

Situation

A co-leader and I disagreed on whether to prioritize rapid event expansion or deepen quality for existing Fellows. The disagreement started to affect meeting tone and delayed decisions.

Action

I requested a dedicated resolution session instead of debating in fragmented messages. We each outlined assumptions, success metrics, and risks. Then we built a two-path comparison matrix across capacity, Fellow experience, and long-term sustainability.

Compromise

We agreed to a phased approach: maintain high-quality programming for current Fellows while piloting one additional event with clear quality thresholds and a retrospective checkpoint.

Learning

I learned that strong collaboration is not about avoiding disagreement; it is about creating a process where both people feel heard, decisions are evidence-based, and trust is protected throughout execution.
